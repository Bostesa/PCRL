\title{Task-Directed Releases Beside a Fixed Prediction Service:\\
Measuring Additional Sensitive Recovery for Single and Colluding Recipients}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Submission}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
An organisation already publishes a prediction service whose outputs downstream recipients consume and
cannot be asked to re-accept. We study what it can release \emph{beside} those fixed outputs for one
recipient: a channel that must serve a permitted task while limiting what that recipient, and that
recipient colluding with another, can newly infer about sex and race. We formalise this purpose-specific
release problem, give it an implemented interface and measurement contract---per-recipient and coalition
views, independently fitted attackers, additional disclosure always reported against the fixed service
baseline, and negative increments retained unclipped---and use it to evaluate a task-directed finite
release optimised under explicit conditional-disclosure constraints. On repeatedly used development data,
the selected release improves permitted-task loss over the strongest channel this project had by
$0.0163$ nats with paired household bounds excluding zero, and all eight sensitive point estimates favour
it; but seven of the eight adjusted non-inferiority bounds do not resolve, so the registered
competitiveness conjunction is \textbf{not} established, and a matched deterministic release without the
stochastic machinery performs comparably. We then evaluate two frozen operating points prospectively on a
previously unused survey year under a lock created before any final label was read. Both pass all eight
registered sensitive-recovery clauses against the prior channel and both fail both task clauses: their
task upper bounds are below zero but above the registered $0.003$-nat minimum, so each passes eight of ten
clauses and neither establishes the full claim. Against matched randomisation and withholding controls the
randomised release buys task loss at the price of significantly higher measured recovery on six of eight
endpoints, so no frontier advantage for randomisation is established. We report what the comparison does not isolate: the task
objective used authorised supervision the comparator never received, and neither randomisation nor
coalition conditioning earned its registered attribution. We also correct published claims of our own,
including a linear-erasure guarantee that a counterexample refutes, and state precisely which guarantees
are finite-model rather than population statements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A bank collects mortgage applications and uses them for several purposes. An underwriter must not see
applicant race; a fair-lending auditor must. A pricing analyst sits somewhere in between. Each purpose
admits a different set of permissible attributes, and this is the dilemma of \emph{purpose limitation}
codified in data-protection regulation and, at the representation level, of contextual integrity.

Most of the machine-learning literature attacks this problem by owning the representation: it edits,
projects or retrains until an attribute is hard to recover. In deployment the harder version is often the
one where **the organisation has already published something**. A prediction service is live, its outputs
are inside the recipients' systems, and they cannot be recalled or renegotiated. The only decision left is
what to place \emph{beside} the fixed outputs, for whom, and how to measure what that addition gives away.

This paper formalises and evaluates that release problem. A service publishes probability vectors $H_A$ to
one recipient and $H_B$ to another; a mechanism may add a channel for the first recipient only. The
permitted task is a residence-mobility prediction that recipient is authorised to perform; the protected
targets are sex and race, forbidden to both recipients individually and to the two acting together.

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{The release problem and its measurement contract} (\S\ref{sec:problem}): per-recipient and

\section{The release problem and the measurement contract}
\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{Views} Each person has covariates $X$, sensitive attributes $S$ (sex, 2 classes; race, 9
classes) and task labels. Two services are frozen and published first: $H_A(X)\in[0,1]^4$ and
$H_B(X)\in[0,1]^2$. A mechanism may add a channel $Z$ for $A$ only, so $A$ holds $(H_A,Z)$, $B$ holds
$H_B$, and the coalition holds $(H_A,H_B,Z)$. The policy authorises $A$ for the residence task and
forbids sex and race to $A$, to $B$ and to the coalition.

\paragraph{Threat model} The adversary holds labelled data from the release year, fits attack families on
its own pool, selects on a validation pool, and sees only its legal view. It knows the mechanism, the
codebook and the channel law; what it does not hold is the person-specific draw. Attacks are fitted on the
\emph{unrounded continuous} service predictions, never on a coarsened view, because any conditioning
partition used inside an optimiser is a property of that optimiser and not of the released interface.

\paragraph{Single-token semantics} The released object is one sampled token per person, drawn once and
reused. Publishing the channel row itself, or redrawing per query, is a different mechanism with different
disclosure, and we never score one as if it were the other. Expected scoring averages \emph{losses} over
the token distribution, not predicted probabilities; the two differ, and only the first is the expectation
of what a recipient experiences.

\paragraph{What a number means here} Every recovery figure is the log-loss reduction an independently
fitted, validation-selected attacker achieves relative to the same family restricted to $H$. Absolute
recovery---the entropy baseline minus the held-out loss of an attacker fixed before evaluation---is, up
to sampling error, a lower bound on the view's mutual information with the attribute. The increment over
the $H$-restricted fit is a difference of two such lower bounds and bounds $I(S;Z\mid H)$ in neither
direction~\cite{mcallester2020formal}: a restricted predictor can improve after receiving a deterministic
feature $Z=f(H)$ even where $I(S;Z\mid H)=0$, and a failed attack bounds nothing from above.

\section{What linear audits do and do not certify}
\label{sec:audit}

The evaluation contract above is a response to a concrete measurement failure we documented in this line
of work, and the failure is worth stating because it motivates every audit choice that follows.

\paragraph{Aggregate scores hide rare-class leakage} A common compliance reading is an aggregate one-hot
regression score $R^2(h,A)$ against a multi-class protected attribute. For an imbalanced attribute this
aggregate is a prevalence-weighted convex combination of the per-class one-versus-rest scores,
\[
R^2_{\mathrm{onehot}}=\textstyle\sum_k w_k R^2_{\mathrm{OvR},k},\qquad
w_k=\frac{\pi_k(1-\pi_k)}{\sum_j \pi_j(1-\pi_j)},
\]
so a rare class contributes almost nothing to the number a compliance report prints. This is the
familiar variance-weighted multi-output $R^2$, not a new identity; it holds exactly when both scores share
rows, centring and ridge penalty. Auditing the \emph{dominant axis} instead---the largest per-class
one-versus-rest score, itself only a lower bound on the best linear sensitive direction---exposes what the
aggregate smooths away. On the imbalanced mortgage attribute the gap is large (Table~\ref{tab:da}): mean
$R^2_{\mathrm{DA}}=0.0609$ against $R^2_{\mathrm{onehot}}=0.0192$ over nine multi-class pair-seeds, and in
one configuration the aggregate reads $0.027$ where the dominant axis reads $0.288$, on the rarest
class (prevalence $0.9\%$), for which the aggregate alone cannot bound the class score. Replaying the
stored per-class scores reproduces the stored aggregate within $2\times10^{-5}$ in $31$ of $33$ cells; the
two exceptions trace to a single-precision aggregate in the historical evaluation code.

\paragraph{Three limits we now state explicitly} First, \textbf{a zero linear score does not bound
classification}. We previously asserted that zero affine least-squares $R^2$ forces every linear threshold
classifier to majority accuracy. That is false, and a twenty-row counterexample settles it: both
conditional feature means are zero, so $\mathrm{Cov}(h,A)=0$ and the optimal affine predictor is constant
with $R^2=0$, yet thresholding at $h>0$ classifies $18/20$ correctly against a $50\%$ majority baseline.
The claim is withdrawn and the corresponding API refuses to run in the evaluated code. The valid
statement concerns losses convex in an affine prediction: zero cross-covariance means no affine predictor
improves on the best constant under squared, logistic or any other such loss on that
distribution~\cite{belrose2023leace}; thresholded accuracy is not such a loss.

Second, \textbf{composition is exact only at exact zero}. Two views with exactly zero cross-covariance
with the same signal retain exactly zero under concatenation, yet can determine it nonlinearly: with
independent signs $V,S$, the views $SV$ and $V$ each have zero covariance with $S$ and their product is
$S$. Approximate leakage composes with a conditioning penalty: for views with nonsingular covariances,
$R^2(h_1,\dots,h_k)\le\sum_p R^2(h_p)/\lambda_{\min}(R)$, where $R$ is the block-whitened cross-view
correlation (a Rayleigh--Ritz bound). It is tight: $h_1=N+\delta S$ and $h_2=N-\delta S$ each carry
$R^2=\delta^2/(1+\delta^2)$, $\lambda_{\min}(R)=2\delta^2/(1+\delta^2)$, and their difference recovers $S$.

Third, \textbf{more retained rank is not more task performance, and fewer flagged attacks is not low
absolute disclosure}. We report absolute recovery beside every increment for this reason.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Erasure the releaser owns} Linear concept erasure gives an exact guarantee against linear
adversaries on a representation the releaser may edit~\cite{belrose2023leace}, and task-relevant variants
preserve authorised covariance~\cite{holstege2025splince}. Adversarial fair and transferable
representations are due to Madras et al.~\cite{madras2018laftr}; adversarial removal is known to leave
recoverable information~\cite{elazar2018adversarial}, and least-privilege learning has fundamental
limits~\cite{stadler2024leastprivilege}. The closed-form line of Sadeghi et
al.~\cite{sadeghi2019sarl,sadeghi2021optnetarl,sadeghi2022ktopt} and U-FaTE~\cite{dehdashtian2024ufate},
which conditions its dependence measure on the target label, solve the same shape of problem when the
representation is the releaser's to change. Flexibly fair representations let each task drop a different
subset of sensitive attributes from one shared encoder~\cite{creager2019ffvae}. All of these assume what our setting denies: here the published output is immutable
and the only decision is what to append.

\paragraph{Finite-alphabet privacy--utility optimisation} The convex programme of \S\ref{sec:method} is
established: finite-alphabet leakage--distortion design is convex for a fixed joint
law~\cite{calmon2012privacy}, including after quantising to a finite code~\cite{salamatian2015managing}.
Rassouli and G\"und\"uz~\cite{rassouli2021perfect} characterise when perfect privacy is feasible through a
nullspace condition and reduce the zero-leakage problem with mutual-information utility to a linear
programme; our zero-budget case is conditional and is linear by the same argument. The privacy funnel of
Makhdoumi et al.~\cite{makhdoumi2014funnel} is nonconvex precisely because of a utility constraint we do
not impose. A new release has been designed beside fixed earlier releases under an incremental-leakage
budget~\cite{erdogdu2015continual}, and per-party and collusion mutual-information constraints on
sequential releases were studied recently~\cite{taylor2026collusion}; since $C$ is fixed,
$I(S;Z\mid C)=I(S;Z,C)-I(S;C)$, so our conditional budgets are joint-view budgets with a shifted
constant. Side information at the recipient appears in~\cite{sankar2013utility,liao2019sideinfo}, and
randomised finite pre-processing under fairness constraints in~\cite{calmon2017optimized}. We claim no new
optimisation result. What is ours is the setting---the conditioning view is a third party's published
prediction service that a coalition partner also holds---the measurement contract, and an evaluated
operating point. Related conditional-independence and information-bound machinery we rely on for scoping
comes from the kernel conditional-independence literature~\cite{zhang2011kci,strobl2019rcit}, cross-fitted
nuisance estimation~\cite{chernozhukov2016dml}, and the formal limits of mutual-information
estimation~\cite{mcallester2020formal}. The data are Folktables-style ACS microdata~\cite{ding2021retiring}.

\section{A task-directed finite release}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Construction} Two residence-supervised teachers, $p(X_A,H_A)$ and $b(H_A)$, define a residual
logit $r=\operatorname{logit}p-\operatorname{logit}b$; its $32$ quantile cells form a code $T=g(X_A,H_A)$,
into which no protected label enters at runtime. $T$ is mapped to a finite output alphabet by a
row-stochastic channel $Q$, and the recipient receives one sampled token. Writing
$C_A=H_A$ and $C_{AB}=(H_A,H_B)$ for the conditioning views, the programme is
\[
\min_{Q\ \ge\ 0,\ Q\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}}\ \sum_{t,z}p(t)\,Q_{tz}D_{tz}
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad I_Q(S_r;Z\mid C_r)\le\delta_r ,
\]
with a cost table $D$ fixed before optimisation. Under the mechanism's Markov structure $Z-T-(S,C)$ and a
fixed fitted law, each conditional mutual information is affine in $Q$ inside a jointly convex relative
entropy, so the feasible set is convex and the objective linear. A \emph{local} policy imposes the $r=A$
constraints only; a \emph{coalition} policy adds $r=AB$. The selected utility release is local, so its
coalition view is audited but not constrained. We report solver status and replay primal feasibility;
no duality gap was recorded, so optimality of the fitted programme is solver-reported, not certified.

\paragraph{Attribution, honestly} This is an \emph{established} convex programme applied to a new release
contract, not a new optimisation result. The convex finite-alphabet leakage--distortion
problem~\cite{calmon2012privacy}, releases beside fixed earlier releases~\cite{erdogdu2015continual},
collusion constraints~\cite{taylor2026collusion} and the nullspace feasibility
criterion~\cite{rassouli2021perfect} are prior work; the privacy funnel is nonconvex precisely because of
a utility constraint we do not impose. Optimising the decoder jointly with
$Q$, or maximising a task mutual information, destroys the convexity, and we do neither. What is ours is
the release contract, the per-recipient and coalition constraint structure, and the evaluated operating
point.

\paragraph{What the constraint is, and is not} $\delta_r$ binds a conditional mutual information in a
\emph{fitted finite model} whose conditioning view is a coarsened partition---two cells of $H_A$ for
$A$, four of $(H_A,H_B)$ for $AB$. It is not a population
guarantee and not a statement about the continuous service vectors: in a validated fixture, binning a
conditioning view drops a full $\log 2$ of measured leakage to exactly zero. All audited attacks therefore
use continuous $H$, and the budgets are never quoted as measured increments over the prior channel.

\paragraph{Why a replacement can beat an extension} An exact fixture bounds what any ``does the new
channel add anything?'' test can conclude. With $H$ constant, a prior channel $J=(Y,S)$ carrying both the
useful bit and the sensitive bit, and a candidate $T=Y$: appending $T$ to $J$ adds exactly zero task
information, while \emph{replacing} $J$ by $T$ preserves the task in full and removes the sensitive bit
entirely ($\log 2$ nats). A test that measures the first can return zero while the second is strictly
better. This is a design example, not an ACS finding.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\paragraph{Setup} California ACS one-year public-use microdata, ages 19--34, household-separated pools for
representation fitting, task fitting and validation, attacker fitting and validation, and evaluation.
Uncertainty is a paired household bootstrap over $5{,}473$ households, aggregated across three anchors that
share people, with simultaneous Bonferroni-adjusted bounds over a frozen family of $706$ contrasts
($z=3.97$). All numbers in this section are \textbf{development} evidence on repeatedly used pools and are
conditional on the fitted releases.

\paragraph{The selected release improves the permitted task} The utility-route nominee $Q$ reduces
residence log loss relative to the strongest channel this project had, $J$, by $0.01625$ nats unweighted
and $0.01639$ person-weighted, with adjusted bounds excluding zero in both weightings
(Table~\ref{tab:bounds}, Figure~\ref{fig:bounds}).

\paragraph{The competitiveness conjunction is not established} All eight sensitive point estimates favour
$Q$ over $J$, but only one adjusted non-inferiority bound resolves ($AB$/sex unweighted, upper
$-0.00007$). The registered conjunction therefore fails. \textbf{This is not evidence of excess
disclosure}: seven endpoints are unresolved at this precision, and an unresolved interval is not a finding
in either direction.

\paragraph{A simpler baseline matches it} $D_{17}$ uses the same code and the same $17$-action dictionary
with no privacy constraint and no stochastic optimisation. It attains a slightly \emph{better} task point
estimate than $Q$ and \emph{lower} sensitive point estimates in seven of eight cells (Table~\ref{tab:rel}).
All ten corresponding adjusted differences are unresolved, so we do not claim $D_{17}$ is better, and we
equally do not claim the two are equivalent. $D_{33}$, with a larger dictionary, has better task point
estimates and mixed sensitive comparisons; its cap feasibility was not established and it is not a
dominating method. The honest reading is that on this evidence \textbf{the stochastic machinery has not
been shown to buy anything over a matched deterministic release}.

\paragraph{Baselines, and what each one was allowed to see} The comparison set is deliberately wider than
$J$. \emph{Deterministic action releases} ($D_{17}$, $D_{33}$) use the same residence-supervised code and action
dictionary with no privacy constraint. A \emph{direct code} release publishes the code index itself. A
\emph{continuous task prediction} publishes the real-valued authorised prediction and is the strongest task
point in the table, with the worst sensitive comparison of the group. \emph{Supervised erasure adaptations}
(LEACE and SPLINCE) were fitted with protected labels on the same standardised inputs plus the clipped
frozen residence-teacher logit; SPLINCE additionally preserves residence, income and employment covariance,
so it sees authorised task labels that $J$ never saw. Two fitting pools were prepared in advance---one
matching the mechanism's own fresh-label rows and one the broader union---so that no post-outcome choice
between fairness interpretations was needed, with each method's own complete-case mask disclosed rather
than assumed identical. These adaptations completed, but neither supplied an \emph{eligible} nominee for
the utility route, which is why the stricter all-family diagnostic is reported as blocked rather than as
passed or failed. An unavailable comparator family is not a verdict about that family.

\paragraph{What the comparison does not isolate} $Q$'s objective used authorised residence supervision.
$J$ never received that training objective. The comparison therefore establishes neither unseen-task
transfer nor an isolated benefit of the new optimiser, and a supervised task result must not be described
as an untouched-task result. The continuous task predictor and the supervised eraser adaptations are
reported with their actual label access and pools for the same reason.

\paragraph{Neither refinement earned its claim} Neither risk refinement nor coalition conditioning obtained
its registered attribution: the nominated release is \emph{local}. Beating $J$ alone cannot attribute a
benefit to the coalition machinery.

\paragraph{Why an earlier explanation was wrong} A predecessor experiment found that a label-free code of
the permitted inputs added nothing measurable once the published predictions were in the recipient's view,
and read this as the published output already carrying the available signal. A subsequent controlled
diagnosis separated the two candidate explanations and found the opposite: the \emph{unquantized} input
view attains strictly lower residence loss than $J$, with adjusted bounds below zero, while a $64$-state
code of the same inputs gives that advantage away. The obstacle was quantization, not redundancy, and the
earlier universal reading is withdrawn (\S\ref{sec:corrections}).

\section{A prospective test on a previously unused year}
\label{sec:prospective}

Everything above is development evidence on pools this project has reused. We therefore froze two
operating points---the randomised release $Q$ and the matched deterministic release $D_{17}$---and
evaluated them on a California ACS 2016 cohort that no earlier stage had touched: 23{,}684 people in
15{,}928 households, with the code, release laws, attacker and probe recipes, fitting selections,
estimands and inference thresholds locked before any final-partition label was read. No mechanism was
fitted or selected on 2016 outcomes. The registered primary claim is a ten-clause conjunction: two task
clauses requiring an uncertainty-adjusted improvement of at least $0.003$ nats over $J$, and eight
sensitive-recovery clauses capping added recovery at $+0.001$ nats.

\textbf{Both releases fail the conjunction, and both fail it in the same way} (Table~\ref{tab:p2016},
Figure~\ref{fig:p2016}). Each passes \textbf{all eight} sensitive clauses: every upper bound lies below
zero, which is stronger than the registered cap. Each fails \textbf{both} task clauses. $Q$'s task upper
bounds are $-0.001505$ and $-0.001871$; $D_{17}$'s are $-0.002913$ and $-0.002951$; the registered
requirement was $\le-0.003$. So each release passes eight of ten clauses and neither establishes the
complete claim.

\textbf{The distinction matters and follows from the same bounds.} Both task upper bounds are strictly
below zero, so the data do support \emph{a} task improvement over $J$ on this fresh year; what they do not
support is the \emph{registered minimum} improvement. That is a statement about the size of the effect
relative to a margin fixed in advance, not a rescue of the claim by a new criterion, and the margin is not
relaxed here. The margin itself was not unreachable: a precision projection made before scoring, from
archived development losses, put the attainable standard error at $0.0009$--$0.0016$ (the realised value
was $0.0009$--$0.0011$) and the pass probability near one \emph{if the development effect size carried
over}. It did not. The development improvement over $J$ was $-0.0163$ nats; the prospective estimate is
$-0.0033$, about a fifth of it. \textbf{The conjunction failed because the effect shrank on a fresh year,
not because the test was underpowered for the effect it expected}---which is the most informative thing
this evaluation produced, and a caution about development effect sizes generally. Equally, the eight passing sensitive clauses are a real result on this attacker slate and
these access views---not a universal privacy guarantee, not evidence about arbitrary attackers, and not a
coalition-specific property.

\textbf{Against matched simple controls, no frontier advantage appears.} In a separate,
multiplicity-corrected family of $70$ endpoints, $Q$ has lower task loss than a $75\%$ randomised-response
control ($-0.011652$, $-0.011170$) and a $75\%$ withholding control ($-0.007055$, $-0.006875$) under both
weightings---but it also has \emph{significantly higher} measured sensitive recovery on \textbf{six of
eight} endpoints against each. A complete task-and-privacy benefit over these controls is therefore not
established, and task performance alone does not imply dominance. Against the deterministic $D_{17}$ and
$D_{33}$, $Q$ is worse on unweighted task loss and shows no significant sensitive advantage on any
endpoint, so \textbf{randomisation is not shown to beat the matched deterministic control}. Direct
supervised and erasure-adaptation controls achieve better task loss than $Q$ at different measured
disclosure costs.

\textbf{What this evaluation is not.} The frozen release family was built with task and sensitive labels
during earlier development, so this is a fresh-year evaluation of a \emph{supervised} fixed release
family, not evidence of transfer to an unseen task. It does not replace the 2018 development programme's
own failed decision, and the two are reported separately throughout. The panel completed without candidate
replacement or outcome-driven stopping: $156/156$ fit units, $156/156$ score units, zero unit failures,
zero quarantined scores, $10{,}000/10{,}000$ accepted bootstrap draws. An independent replay reproduced
every stored per-person expected loss to $3.6\times10^{-15}$ and all $204$ point estimates to
$1.4\times10^{-17}$, and the published service outputs were byte-identical across all $30$ interfaces.

\section{Limitations, and a direct answer}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Development versus prospective evidence.} The $2018$ pools have been used repeatedly across this
line of work; three anchors and simultaneous intervals do not restore independence from prior research.
The $2016$ evaluation of \S\ref{sec:prospective} is prospective and locked, but it evaluates a release
family whose teachers and code were built with labels during that earlier development, so it is a
fresh-year test of a supervised fixed family rather than an unseen-task result. The two bodies of
evidence are never pooled.

\textbf{Failing a conjunction is not a null.} Both $2016$ releases failed both task clauses while their
task upper bounds sat below zero; that is a shortfall against a pre-registered margin, not a demonstration
that the task effect is zero. Conversely, passing eight sensitive clauses is a measured result for the
registered attacker slate, roles and access views, not a guarantee against other attackers.

\textbf{Unresolved is not equivalence, and not harm.} Seven of eight sensitive comparisons are unresolved.
We do not convert them into either claim.

\textbf{Fitted-model budgets are not population guarantees}, coarse conditioning is not conditioning on the
continuous service vectors, and negative finite-attacker increments do not demonstrate negative conditional
mutual information.

\textbf{Attacks bound from below only.} Three searches for a stronger attacker across this project each
failed to beat the standard slate, which bounds what those searches found, not what exists.

\textbf{Audits do not transport across hardware} below about $0.003$ nats, so comparisons use same-host
references.

\paragraph{What was established} A task-directed release beside a fixed service improved the permitted task
over the strongest channel we had, with bounds excluding zero on development data; and on a previously
unused year, two frozen operating points each passed all eight registered sensitive-recovery clauses
against that channel, with task improvements whose upper bounds were below zero. \textbf{What was not
established}: the registered conjunction in either year---on $2016$ both releases missed the $0.003$-nat
task margin---any advantage of the stochastic machinery over a matched deterministic release, any complete
benefit over matched randomisation or withholding controls, any coalition-specific benefit, and any
unseen-task claim.

\section{Relation to the earlier encoder line}
\label{sec:lineage}

This work descends from a purpose-conditioned representation line in which one shared frozen, randomly
initialised backbone carried per-purpose adapters, each warm-started from a closed-form erasure projection~\cite{belrose2023leace} and trained under a
per-purpose linear leakage bound. That setting differs from this one in its release contract: there the
releaser owned and re-emitted the representation, here the service output is immutable and the decision is
what to append. The auditing result of \S\ref{sec:audit} and the composition limits come from that line and
survive checking; its training results are not re-used as evidence for the present mechanism, because they
tested a different algorithm against a different claim. Appendix~\ref{app:lineage} gives the correspondence.

\section{Lineage and what each study tested}
\label{app:lineage}
The encoder line and the present release line share an evaluation philosophy and a protected-attribute
audit, not an algorithm. Claims from the encoder line used here are limited to the dominant-axis audit, the
convex-combination identity, the retired guarantee and the composition boundary. The encoder line's
training headline totals are same-grid post hoc development evidence: the dual floor and warm-up rule were
developed after examining an earlier evaluation on the same grid, and neither was validated on a held-out
subset of purpose/attribute/seed triples. A later prospective registration does not retrospectively change
that scope.

Two further scope facts belong with those totals. \emph{Checkpoint rule.} Counts differ by which
checkpoint is scored: the final iterate gives $56/60$ cells meeting the strict linear criterion, of which
$7$ also clear a non-collapse health check, while a per-seed best-checkpoint rule gives $54/60$; a later
erase-layer arm reaches $60/60$ strict but $0/60$ clean. Strict and clean counts are reported separately
and under one stated rule, never mixed. \emph{Untested components.} Whether the low-rank adapter or the
frozen erasure layer is \emph{necessary} was registered as two ablations that were never run, so no
necessity or advantage is claimed for either; the one comparison that exists confounds the erasure layer
with the constraint and cannot separate them. Neither this paper nor the release mechanism it evaluates
shares an implementation with that line: no combined encoder, adapter and stochastic-release pipeline has
been built or evaluated, and the two are reported as separate implementations throughout.

\section{Corrections this paper makes}
\label{sec:corrections}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{The universal regression-to-classification guarantee is withdrawn.} Zero affine $R^2$ can
coexist with $90\%$ threshold accuracy; the valid statement is the narrower squared-loss one
(\S\ref{sec:audit}).
\item \textbf{A shared union eraser does not demonstrate retention of conflicting purposes.} In the
recorded comparison the shared union destroys both tasks ($R^2\approx0$ on each), while separate
per-purpose erasure retains both ($0.9996$, $0.9997$); the restricted arm ties that comparator rather than
beating it.
\item \textbf{``The published output already carries the signal''} is withdrawn: a controlled diagnosis
attributes the effect to quantization, not redundancy (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
\item \textbf{A proposed Sadeghi--Boddeti near-optimality certificate is not an implemented result.}
It was explicitly skipped for want of the necessary numerical check and is not claimed here.
\item \textbf{The encoder line's backbone was not pretrained.} It was described as pretrained on the
union of permitted tasks; in the implementation that produced its results it was a seeded random MLP,
frozen at initialisation.
\item \textbf{A rank floor for single-layer edits is not a floor for the trained adapters.} Erasure below
$\sum_{i>r}\sigma_i^2/\operatorname{tr}\operatorname{Cov}(A)$ is impossible for one rank-$r$
multiplicative edit of a fixed representation, but the trained adapters edit every layer with
nonlinearities in between; a rank-one edit of the first layer can remove a rank-two sensitive
cross-covariance entirely.
\item \textbf{Exact-zero covariance composition} holds; approximate leakage does not inherit it, and
neither implies independence or a nonlinear guarantee.
\end{enumerate}
