# What this work does, in one paragraph

PCRL began as a way to let one shared encoder serve several purposes at once, giving each purpose its own
adapter so that an attribute a purpose may not use is linearly hard to recover from that purpose's
representation. This paper keeps that problem but changes the contract to the one that actually binds in
deployment: the organisation has **already published** a prediction service, its outputs are inside the
recipients' systems and cannot be recalled, and the only remaining decision is what to release *beside*
them for one recipient — measured against what that recipient, and that recipient colluding with a second,
can newly infer about sex and race. What is technically distinctive here is not the optimiser, which is an
established finite-alphabet convex programme, but the release contract and the measurement: per-recipient
and coalition audit roles, attackers fitted independently on the unrounded published outputs, additional
disclosure always reported against the fixed service baseline, one sampled token as the released object,
and negative increments kept rather than clipped. What was measured: on repeatedly used 2018 development
data, the selected task-directed release improves the permitted residence task over the strongest channel
this project had by 0.0163 nats with paired household bounds excluding zero, and all eight sensitive point
estimates move in the favourable direction. The strongest missing claim is the one the registration asked
for: seven of those eight adjusted non-inferiority bounds do not resolve, so the competitiveness
conjunction is **not** established, a matched deterministic release without the stochastic machinery
performs comparably, the task objective used authorised supervision the comparator never received, and
neither randomisation nor coalition conditioning earned its registered attribution.
